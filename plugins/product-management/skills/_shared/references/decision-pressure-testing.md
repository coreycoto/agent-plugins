# Decision pressure-testing

Use this reference when deciding whether an idea deserves a commitment, when
apparently supportive evidence conflicts, or when choosing which assumption to
test first. A settled, supported requirement does not need a fresh discovery
exercise merely because this reference exists.

## Identify the assumption that can reverse the decision

State the proposed choice and its intended user outcome. Trace the causal chain
from the observed problem through the proposed behavior to that outcome. Find
the assumption whose failure would make the commitment wasteful or change the
preferred option. A weak fact matters here because of its consequence, not
because every missing fact deserves investigation.

For each material assumption, retain the supplied evidence, its origin and
limits, conflicting observations, and the decision that depends on it. Use
qualitative reasoning when probability, impact or cost is unknown; do not invent
scores to rank uncertainties. Separate user need, solution usefulness,
feasibility and business viability. Evidence for one does not establish another.

For example, interview enthusiasm for a reporting feature may support interest
while leaving unresolved whether users can obtain the necessary inputs. A
prototype that proves the report is understandable cannot settle that dependency.

## Compare credible alternatives

Compare the proposed solution with relevant existing behavior, a smaller change,
or a different way to meet the same need. Include defer or stop when evidence or
constraints support them. Alternatives should be plausible in the consumer's
context, not weak examples constructed to favor the proposal.

Inspect existing research, support records, actual usage, prior experiments and
constraints before asking questions. Treat repeated accounts derived from one
source as one line of evidence. Seek a source or method that could contradict
the favored explanation: unsuccessful users, abandonment, a failed workaround,
or behavior under a competing option. Preserve sample and selection limits.
Absent complaints do not by themselves establish absent need; preference for an
imagined feature does not establish adoption.

Ground the comparison in known constraints such as accessibility, cost,
integration capability, timing and organizational commitments. Keep an unknown
constraint visible until an owner or source establishes it. Do not turn a
technical prototype into a staffing or delivery promise.

Ask a question only when its answer can change the framing, preferred option,
test or commitment. Explain that connection when helpful. Research factual
answers first; ask the user for their judgment when a value choice remains.
There is no required questionnaire, question count or interview gate.

## Test the consequential claim

Use `product-experiments` to connect the hypothesis to an observation, comparison
and decision. Choose a prototype only if the behavior it exercises addresses the
assumption. A task prototype can test comprehension or usability; a mocked
integration can expose an interaction while leaving real integration feasibility
open. A polished demo supplies no demand evidence by itself.

Record the decision criteria before observing results: what would support
continuing, changing direction, deferring or stopping, and what would remain
inconclusive. Preserve unknown numeric thresholds as unknown. A justified
qualitative criterion can be useful, but it must describe observable behavior
and its decision consequence. If that criterion needs the decision owner's
choice, record the unresolved choice rather than inventing it.

Check whether the method can produce a disconfirming result. A test designed so
that every response supports building is not an informative test. Identify
confounds and the limits of what this observation can establish. Collection,
customer contact and live changes follow the task's existing authorization.

## Return a decision the owner can inspect

Use a compact brief or comparison suited to the task. Connect the choice,
credible alternatives, consequential assumption, supporting and disconfirming
evidence, proposed test and criteria. Link source material rather than replacing
it with an unsupported confidence label. Make the recommendation conditional
where appropriate, including a defensible defer or stop recommendation.

The owner should be able to explain why this option is preferred, identify what
could reverse it, and see which learning step would resolve that uncertainty.
This is a comprehension check, not a claim of measured confidence or product
success.
